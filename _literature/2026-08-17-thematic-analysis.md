## Statement card analysis

During my studies, I learned to do qualitative analysis by transcribing interviews, highlighting interesting quotes, writing an interpretation and then (with other researchers) cluster them into themes. Apparently, that’s called ‘statement card analysis’ (Stappers et al., 2023). More recently, I kept encountering ‘coding’ as a term for (part of) this process. In the [living contracts](./2026-08-13-living-contracts.md) paper, Huang et al. cited Braun and Clarke’s _Using thematic analysis in psychology_. With over 140 000 citations, apparently that’s a hit. 

## Thematic analysis overlap

The approach is similar: get familiar with the data (by transcribing and reading), create initial codes (basically a statement card), search for themes (clustering), review, find and name the themes, and then report on it. Both this paper and Stappers et al. refer to grounded theory research, so I’ll look into that, too.

## Some new things

There were some things in Braun and Clarke’s paper which made sense and/or I wanted to remind myself of:
- Writing and analysis are parallel processes
- Themes don’t ‘emerge’ passively; as a research you actively find, select and report on them
- The importance (‘keyness’) of a theme depends on its relation to the research question
- The tips to:
  - …code for as many themes as you can find
  - …keep some surrounding context in the quote/highlight
  - …remember that quotes can fit multiple themes
- They agree a miscellaneous theme is useful while initially clustering
- Themes make sense when they’re based on actual data (duh), coherent as a unit, and different from the other themes 
- Good theme names are short and descriptive on their own
- The reporting combines the themes into a coherent story which answers the research question(s), with a nice amount of data points to back it up 

## Important questions for coding

- Do you report the reality of participants (essentialist/realist), report how they fit within society (constructionist), or do you sit in between and do both (contextualist)?
  - I needed to figure out what essentialist, realist, constructionist and contextualist mean. I feel like a constructionist take (qualities are produced by context, i.e. nurture, not nature) on life often makes a lot of sense, but I don’t really feel qualified to actually link participants’ statements to societal currents.
- Do you focus on what the participant said (semantic), or do you try to figure out the underlying ideas and ideologies (latent)?
  - Braun and Clarke also mention that the latent focus matches with a constructionist take. I think that, maybe, for design research, a semantic and essentialist approach is fine.
- How does the creation of a theme relate to how often it is mentioned, by how many people, how strongly, and with how many words?
- Do you get a specific set of themes from the data related to narrow question(s), or do you describe the themes more broadly?
  - In design research, I feel like the former often makes sense.
- Do you find statements with themes you expect (deductive), or from whatever you find in the data (inductive)?
  - Currently, I’m asking questions about values people might attach to elections (fits nicely with deductive) and figuring out what problems they encounter with my prototype (fits nicely with inductive).
